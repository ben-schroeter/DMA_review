Methodology

Here you will find a description of how The EU Digital Rulebook was built. The technical documentation, code and validation samples are in the data download.

1. Scope

The list started from the inventory of EU digital law and policy by Zenner and Marcus (2026), including eight enacted instruments listed in the same table, and is kept up to date by this project. It covers laws governing digital technologies, products, services, data, platforms, cybersecurity, electronic communications, AI, digital identity, digital content and digital infrastructure, including online offences and data protection by public authorities, together with the general economic laws (competition, consumer, tax, IP, company and product law) that set key rules for digital markets. Funding programmes, industrial and crisis instruments, and laws on the internal administration of public bodies are not included. Proposals still being negotiated are tracked on the Forthcoming page and enter the counts once adopted. We measured the original act as published in the Official Journal. English versions from EUR-Lex. Each law sits in one policy area. We started from the areas in Zenner and Marcus's table and regrouped them for this scope: product safety and compliance rules (product, machinery, toy, medical device, vehicle and electrical equipment law) are separated from AI and digital trust (the AI Act, eIDAS and the Single Digital Gateway); industrial policy and research no longer stand alone once funding and industrial instruments left the scope, so their remaining laws moved to connectivity and digital infrastructure and to finance; law enforcement is shown as online crime and law enforcement.

2. Counting obligations

An obligation is a legal rule that requires, forbids or limits something. We count the markers that create them in the articles and annexes (recitals are not binding):

  • Positive obligations: shall, must, has to
  • Prohibitions: shall not, may not, is prohibited, "no … shall"
  • Restricted permissions: may only, may … only if
  • Duties of result: shall ensure
  • Explicit requirements: is required to, is obliged to
Counted: "Gatekeepers shall not process personal data of end users…"
Not counted: "This Regulation shall enter into force on…" (it regulates no one)

Excluded uses of "shall": entry into force, definitions, amendment instructions, saving clauses, rights and powers, and constitutive rules such as how a board is composed. Each obligation is assigned to who must act, using the grammatical subject: private parties, member states, EU institutions, or unclear.

How accurate is it? In a fresh hand-checked sample of 200 counted items, 89% were genuine obligations, and the addressee was correct for 90% of those. Totals are reliable enough for comparisons of this size; addressee shares are approximate.

Who must act, rechecked (October 2026): each obligation gets a formal addressee (who the text tells to act) and an "ultimately bound" one (for duties on Member States whose content is company conduct). Where the sentence itself does not name the actor, the rules look at the list lead-in, the previous sentences of the article, the article heading and the act's definitions; the remaining cases (about 5,700 distinct clauses) were read by a model with a logged reason for each. Rules on content or legal effect that bind nobody are coded "no actor". No obligation is left unclear.

3. Reporting obligations

A reporting obligation is one of the counted obligations whose required action is to pass information to someone else, or to create or keep it for others to inspect. Every reporting obligation is therefore also part of the main count. We sort them by the action required:

  • Reports and submissions: report, submit, transmit or provide information or documents
  • Notifications: notify or inform someone of something
  • Information on request: supply information when asked
  • Disclosure: publish, make public, or inform users, consumers, data subjects or the public
  • Registration: register, or enter information in a register, database or system
  • Record-keeping: keep records, logs, documentation or copies for inspection

For each we also record who must act, who receives the information, and how often: periodically, once, when something happens (with any deadline), on request, on an ongoing basis, or not stated in the provision itself.

Counted: "The controller shall notify the personal data breach to the supervisory authority … not later than 72 hours after having become aware of it."
Not counted: "The report shall include …" (it sets a report's content) and "Dates shall be reported in UTC" (format)

Also not counted: bans on disclosure (confidentiality rules), reporting inside a company to its own management body, board or general meeting, and the standard clause requiring member states to adopt and publish their transposing laws.

How accurate is it? The rules were developed on a first sample of 300 obligations, then checked on new samples not used to build them. 197 of 210 obligations counted as reporting were genuine (94%), and 4 of 100 obligations not counted were in fact reporting obligations. The count therefore captures an estimated 83% of them: 6,263 is a lower bound, and the true number is likely about 7,100. These are obligations in the texts, not reports filed.

4. Rules and standards

Every counted obligation is classified as a rule or a standard (Kaplow, 1992). Rule: a fixed action, ban, number, deadline, list or format. Standard: an open term such as appropriate, reasonable, proportionate, fair or effective. Mixed: a fixed duty with a substantive open element. Specified later: the provision says a delegated or implementing act, technical standard or guidelines will set its content. Left to member states: the content is chosen in transposition. Statements that impose no duty are left out, which leaves 20,195 obligations in the main acts and 13,087 in their implementing and delegated acts. In charts, standards include mixed provisions and those to be specified later.

The same obligations are also placed in Coglianese's rule-design framework, on two axes: means or ends (what to do, or what to achieve) and micro or macro (narrow and specific, or broad and general). Management-based duties are the macro-means cell: a duty to set up a process, such as a risk-management system, rather than to reach a result.

Charts by Commission place each law under the Commission that proposed it.

How accurate is it? On a hand-coded check of 200 obligations the rules-and-standards coding agreed 92% of the time; it leans slightly towards "rule", so the shares of standards are about 5 points too low. On 100 obligations coded twice for the rule-design framework, 89 agreed (Cohen's kappa 0.82).

5. Types of company

For each adopted law we coded which of six types of company it applies to (makers of connected products; finance, payments and crypto; gatekeepers and very large platforms; online platforms and digital services; telecoms; cloud, data and AI providers), in full or only in part, from its scope provisions. Where a law has tiers we used its articles: in the Digital Services Act, for example, Articles 11 to 15 apply to all intermediary services and Articles 33 to 43 to very large platforms only. Elsewhere we counted the obligations whose wording names that kind of company. A baseline of rules applies to every company: data protection, consumer law, VAT, company law, competition and intellectual property. These are profiles of types of company, not legal assessments of named firms.

6. Secondary acts and guidance

Implementing and delegated acts are those EUR-Lex records as adopted under each instrument (618 implementing and 194 delegated acts, plus 47 Commission guidance documents and 29 other Commission and Council acts: 888 items, measured like the main acts). With 6 guidance documents found only on Commission websites, that makes 53 Commission guidance documents; the EDPB, EBA and ESMA add 82 guidelines, which are listed but not measured. Only final guidelines are included, not Q&As or drafts. Amendments and national transposition measures come from EUR-Lex metadata.

7. Proposal vs adoption

For 104 adopted acts we compared the original Commission proposal with the adopted text, measured the same way on the legal text only. Six were not compared: the three block exemption regulations, which the Commission adopts without a legislative proposal, and three acts whose proposals were not usable.

8. Impact assessments

For every adopted law we looked up whether the Commission's proposal came with a full impact assessment, using the Commission's document register, EUR-Lex (Cellar) and the Legislative Observatory. Laws are sorted into: full impact assessment (83); proposed before impact assessments began in 2003 (14); codification or recast (5); proposed without one (7); and not established (1). Since 2016 each impact assessment is reviewed by the Regulatory Scrutiny Board; its verdict comes from the published opinion or, for 2016–2019, from the impact assessment's annex. We found it for 36 adopted laws. Impact assessments are shown on each law's page with the objectives, and are not counted as studies.

9. Reviewing the impact evidence

  • Objectives: the objectives, indicators and quantified forecasts in each proposal's financial statement and impact assessment, each checked against the evidence available and given a status.
  • Sources: Commission evaluations, audits, statistics and research on effects on firms, prices, innovation, jobs and consumers. The studies library holds 540 sources: 490 on specific laws, and 50 on the economics of regulation in general (compliance costs, firm size, productivity and innovation), which are not tied to one law. Impact assessments are shown with the objectives; enforcement decisions and court rulings are not counted as evidence. Theory models, legal commentary and opinions written on a proposal before adoption are generally not included, as they cannot show what a law did.
  • Depth: a full literature search for the 29 best-studied instruments and Commission evaluations plus a light search for older acts. In October 2026 a second, broader search covered all 77 acts adopted since 2016: working papers, measurement studies, agency and audit reports, surveys and think tank pieces. It added 189 sources. In November 2026 a third search covered the ten laws added that month, with most attention to the medical device rules, and kept 52 studies, audits and evaluations.
  • Who produced it: every source is labelled academic, official, think tank (including NGOs and consumer organisations), consultancy and industry, or other. Think tank and industry pieces are kept and shown, but labelled, and funders or campaigning positions are flagged.
  • Causal studies (70): those using a design that isolates the law's effect, such as difference-in-differences, event studies, regression discontinuity or synthetic control. Official reviews (126): evaluations, audits and implementation reports by EU and national authorities.
  • Evidence rating: each law is rated on the evidence about its effects, with the reason given on its page. Strong: several causal studies. Moderate: at least one causal study or a solid body of measurement. Thin: mainly the Commission's own reports and descriptive work. Too early to tell: the rules do not yet apply, or apply too recently to measure.
  • Checks: every source was located and its headline checked; each records whether the full text, an abstract or only a summary was read, and flags authors or funders with a stake.

10. Benchmarks and the US comparison

We counted obligations in the Sarbanes-Oxley Act and in US counterparts of EU instruments with the same counter, adapted for US drafting. Precision on US text was 80% (68% for Sarbanes-Oxley). Ten pairs passed our fairness review; the two in which both laws mainly bind public bodies (data protection in the EU institutions, open government data) are not shown, which leaves eight in the comparison with the United States. US words include text inserted into the US Code; EU words are articles and annexes.

11. Use of AI

Claude, an AI model, helped write the counting code, draft the objective summaries and run the literature search; every source and headline figure was checked against the original, and the validation samples were coded by hand.

12. Limitations

  • Counts are not costs: one rule can be trivial, another expensive.
  • Obligations on member states in directives reach companies only after transposition.
  • Counts depend on drafting style: some laws spread one duty over several sentences, others pack several into one.
  • Rules versus standards is a judgement on the wording, not on how strictly a rule is applied in practice.
  • "Not measured" means not found by our searches; search depth differs between laws, and the library is not exhaustive.
  • Studies are counted, not weighted by quality or size.
  • EUR-Lex links for very recent acts can lag.
