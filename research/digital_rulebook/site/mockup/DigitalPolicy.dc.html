Digital Acquis · Digital policy

From market liberalization to digital sovereignty

In 2000 the internet was a niche infrastructure project; by 2010 it was a driver of growth; today it is treated as a question of sovereignty and industrial scale. This page follows that path through six eras of EU policy and places each of the 105 adopted laws in the era in which the Commission proposed it.

Each line is one instrument, from the Commission proposal to adoption; the dot is sized by the number of obligations in the adopted text. Shading marks the eras, by date of proposal; click an era for its summary. Commission terms and portfolios are simplified to the lead commissioner(s) for digital policy.

1987–2000

Market opening

Delors and Santer Commissions · Martin Bangemann (industry, information and telecommunications technologies)

The 1987 Green Paper on telecommunications set the course: open national telecoms monopolies to competition and build one market for equipment and services, a process completed with full liberalisation in 1998. The 1994 Bangemann report framed the “information society” as a project for markets and private investment. The first digital rules were single-market rules: databases, satellite and cable broadcasting, unfair contract terms and, at the end of the decade, electronic commerce and copyright in the information society.

2000–2010

Lisbon

Prodi and first Barroso Commissions · Erkki Liikanen (enterprise and information society), Viviane Reding (information society and media)

The Lisbon European Council set the goal of making the EU “the most competitive and dynamic knowledge-based economy in the world” by 2010. The internet was treated as an enabler for research, education and business, and the eEurope (2000–2005) and i2010 (2005–2010) programmes focused on getting schools, administrations and firms online and on cheaper, faster access. Legislation in this period was mostly sectoral and aimed at consumers: privacy in electronic communications, unfair commercial practices, audiovisual media services, toy safety and consumer rights.

2010–2015

Europe 2020

Second Barroso Commission · Neelie Kroes (Vice-President, Digital Agenda)

After the financial crisis, Europe 2020 made “smart growth” one of three priorities, and the Digital Agenda for Europe was one of its seven flagship initiatives. Targets moved from access to speed: basic broadband for all by 2013, and by 2020 30 Mbps for all and 100 Mbps subscriptions for half of households. The Commission proposed the General Data Protection Regulation in 2012 (adopted 2016), rules on electronic identification and trust services, the second Payment Services Directive and the net-neutrality rules of the Open Internet Regulation.

11 laws adopted · 2,051 obligations · 7 of 10 proposals cite Europe 2020 or the Digital Agenda

Flagships: General Data Protection Regulation (2012→2016) · eIDAS Regulation (2012→2014) · Payment Services Directive 2 (PSD2) (2013→2015) · Open Internet Access Regulation (2013→2015) · See all 11 →

2015–2019

Digital Single Market

Juncker Commission · Andrus Ansip (Vice-President, Digital Single Market), Günther Oettinger, Mariya Gabriel

Goods moved freely across the EU, but digital services ran into 28 national rulebooks. The Digital Single Market strategy set out 16 actions to remove those barriers so that firms could scale across borders. Policy turned to targeted digital legislation: contract rules for digital content and online sales, a ban on unjustified geo-blocking, cross-border portability of online content, copyright for online use, the free flow of non-personal data and a new European Electronic Communications Code. Retail roaming charges ended in 2017, and the GDPR, proposed in the previous era, applied from 2018.

2019–2024

Digital Decade

First von der Leyen Commission · Margrethe Vestager (Executive Vice-President, A Europe Fit for the Digital Age), Thierry Breton (internal market)

The EU came to see its reliance on foreign software, cloud services and chips as a strategic risk. “Shaping Europe’s digital future”, the data strategy and the AI White Paper (all February 2020) launched a wave of horizontal rules; the 2030 Digital Compass and the Digital Decade policy programme added targets for skills, infrastructure, business uptake and public services. This is by far the busiest period: about half of all instruments in the list were proposed in these five years, among them the Digital Markets Act and Digital Services Act for large platforms, the Data Act, the AI Act, the Cyber Resilience Act and the crypto-assets regulation MiCA.

47 laws adopted · 12,297 obligations · 9 more proposals still pending · 27 of 45 proposals cite these strategies

2025–2027

Competitiveness · One Europe, One Market

Second von der Leyen Commission · Henna Virkkunen (Executive Vice-President, Tech Sovereignty, Security and Democracy)

The Draghi report (2024) tied Europe’s lagging productivity to weak digital industries, fragmented markets and high energy costs. The Competitiveness Compass made simplification a priority, with a target to cut businesses’ recurring administrative costs by €37.5 billion by 2029. In April 2026 Parliament, Council and Commission signed the One Europe, One Market roadmap: 42 initiatives in five blocks, among them digital and AI, to be agreed by the end of 2027. So far the era has produced proposals rather than laws: the Digital Omnibus (November 2025) to streamline the data, privacy and cybersecurity rules, the Digital Networks Act and Cybersecurity Act 2 (January 2026), EU Inc (March 2026), the Cloud and AI Development Act and Chips Act 2 (June 2026), and the European Innovation Act (September 2026). The AI part of the Digital Omnibus was adopted in July 2026 as an amendment to the AI Act. The Digital Fairness Act, the Quantum Act and the European Product Act are announced. None of this era’s proposals is law yet, so none is in the counts.

How we assigned the instruments. Each instrument belongs to the era in which the Commission proposed it, whatever the date of adoption; Commission regulations without a legislative proposal (the block exemptions) are placed by date of adoption. To check the link to each era’s strategy we searched the explanatory memorandum, citations and recitals of every Commission proposal available on EUR-Lex for the strategy’s name (for example “Digital Single Market Strategy” or “Shaping Europe’s digital future”). Older proposals rarely name the strategy behind them, so low counts for the early eras say more about drafting habits than about policy. Obligations are counted in the adopted text; proposals still pending are on the Forthcoming page and enter the counts once adopted.
