In Numbers · What companies say

Annual reports show growing importance of EU tech laws

This page analyses which EU tech laws companies flag to their investors. We show how the importance of EU laws has developed over time, and what triggers disclosure. Attention to EU tech laws has grown in two waves: first with the GDPR and then with the DMA, DSA, and AI Act.

2,497annual reports of 134 companies, 2000–2025

How we measured it

The companies are those in the Fortune 100 and in the DAX, CAC 40, FTSE 100 and EURO STOXX 50 indices that are headquartered in the EU, the UK or the US and belong to one of the six types of company (with a few exceptions). Their annual reports come from the US Securities and Exchange Commission (10-K and 20-F filings), from the EU's electronic annual reports (ESEF), and from company and regulator archives. For each report we count every mention of each law, by its name, its abbreviation or its official number, in English, French, German, Italian, Spanish, Dutch and Swedish, per 100,000 words, in the spirit of Hassan, Hollander, van Lent and Tahoun (2019). Samples of matches were checked by hand for every law, and names that also mean other things were excluded.

The set of reports changes over time: companies list, sources change, and a US filing is shorter than a French registration document. So we show two lines: the raw average across all reports each year, and the change within the same companies and sources (a regression with company, year, source and language fixed effects).

Attention has risen in two waves

Until 2015 EU tech law barely featured in annual reports. The GDPR changed that: by 2018 most reports discussed it. A second wave followed from 2020, with the Digital Services Act, the Digital Markets Act, the Digital Operational Resilience Act and the AI Act. The number of EU laws a report names keeps rising, to 3.5 in 2025; within the same companies and sources the rise since 2010 is 3.0 laws. How much companies write about them rose with the GDPR, stayed flat until 2023 and rose again in 2024–25: companies name more laws, but apart from these two steps they do not write proportionally more about each.

Telecoms and gatekeepers name the most EU laws; makers of connected products the fewest

Within the same companies, telecoms operators now name 4.8 more EU laws per report than in 2010 and gatekeepers 4.3 more. Telecoms already discussed EU rules in 2010 (roaming, the telecoms framework); gatekeepers took them up with the platform laws from 2020. Finance caught up from 2018 with payment, operational resilience and crypto-asset rules (3.8). Cloud, data and AI firms waited until the AI Act and the Data Act (2.4), online platforms rose from 2018 (2.1), and makers of connected products rose least (1.6).

US companies talk about the AI Act; European companies about sector rules

Almost every company names the GDPR. US companies name the AI Act more often than European ones (63% against 58%), and as often name the platform and data laws. European companies name a much wider set of sector and compliance rules, from the Digital Operational Resilience Act (45% against 18% of US companies) to the NIS2 cybersecurity directive (30%). UK companies have largely dropped the platform laws: 6% name the Digital Services Act.

Bigger laws get more attention, with clear exceptions

Laws with more obligations on companies are named by more companies. The correlation (on a log scale) is 0.33 across all 110 laws, and 0.43 among the 52 laws that at least two companies name. Size is not the whole story, though: companies talk most about laws that touch their business model or their supervisors. Laws that require companies to run risk-management systems, such as DORA and NIS2, are named more often than their size alone would suggest. The 20 laws named by at least ten companies have a median of 154 obligations, but some of the largest laws are never named at all: the Machinery Regulation (784 obligations), the Toys Safety Regulation and the Radio Equipment Directive. Product rules like these are handled in compliance departments and rarely reach the annual report.

Most laws reach annual reports when they start to apply; the platform laws did so at proposal

Companies typically first name a law when it starts to bite, not when it is adopted. The GDPR was adopted in 2016, but the median company first named it in 2018, the year it began to apply; only 3% of the companies naming NIS2 did so before it was adopted. The Digital Markets Act and the Digital Services Act are the exception: 65% and 52% of the companies naming them did so while they were still proposals, as the firms they target disclosed them as material risks.

Once a law is in the annual report, it stays

84%of laws named in one year's report are named again the next year

Risk-factor and regulation sections are rewritten slowly, and a law that has entered them is rarely taken out. Much of the text is repeated from year to year, so the measure tracks which laws have entered companies' standing account of their risks, not new information each year. It also explains why the number of laws named keeps climbing as new laws arrive.

Three examples

Meta and the GDPR

Meta's 2024 annual report names the GDPR 64 times per 100,000 words, against a median of 4 for reports that name it at all. It describes product changes, consent, data transfers and fines under the regulation in detail.

The gatekeepers and the Digital Markets Act

Apple's mentions of the Digital Markets Act rose from 6 to 41 per 100,000 words between its 2023 and 2024 reports, when the obligations started to apply to the App Store; Meta's rose from 24 to 40.

The AI Act

No law since the GDPR spread as fast. 78 companies, from all six types, named the AI Act within about three years of its proposal, more than the 44 that name the Digital Operational Resilience Act, which binds the whole financial sector.
