About · Why tech regulation matters

Europe's growth problem
is largely a technology problem

Europe’s GDP growth over the past 30 years has lagged behind other regions, particularly the United States. Most of this gap comes from differences in productivity growth. Europe caught up with US productivity in the half-century after the Second World War, then fell behind as the US pulled ahead in information technology. The reason is that the US created and scaled many new technology companies and drove adoption of digital technologies across its economy. Early indicators show that history might repeat itself with AI creation and adoption, where Europe has again fallen behind the US.
This page documents the US-EU productivity gap, explains why technology companies have driven US growth, and reviews what economic research says about the relationship with regulation.

Europe used to have lower GDP per head than the US because it worked less.
Now it is poorer because each hour produces less.

The difference in US-EU GDP per capita can be split into two factors: number of hours worked and output per hour worked. In 1995, the Euro area produced slightly more per hour worked than the US. Its income per head was lower, at 92%, only because people worked fewer hours. Since then the picture has flipped. By 2024 output per hour had fallen to 82% of the US level and GDP per head to 79%, while hours worked per head had moved close to the US level. About 85% of today's gap comes from lower output per hour. 

Technology companies have driven US productivity growth
while Europe has failed to scale its firms

Europe missed out on the first wave of digital technologies, in particular the companies created in the 1990s and scaling after the dot-com bust in the early 2000s. These companies often benefitted from significant scale economies, positively impacting productivity as they grew larger. Economic research attributes about a third of the US-EU productivity gap to the differences in the size and productivity of the digital producing sector. To illustrate the US-EU difference in digital technology creation, Andrew McAfee compares the number and market cap of public companies built from scratch over the past 50 years and which are now worth more than $10 billion. The US has 241 of such companies, together worth about $29.6 trillion, with the most successful ones in digital technologies. In contrast, the EU has only 14, worth $0.43 trillion in total. 

The more a European start-up raises, the more likely it is to leave for the US

Europe’s tech scaling problem isn't just a matter of the past; it  remains an acute challenge, exacerbated by the technological shift to AI. While European tech investment has grown over the last two decades, successful startups keep relocating to the US. Research finds that movement from Europe to the US runs several times higher than the reverse flow across all funding categories. This pull effect strengthens with success: among European firms that had raised more than $100 million, more than 10% moved their headquarters to the US. This matters because headquarters generate well-paying jobs, tax revenue, innovation spillovers, and decisions about where a company grows next. Each move to the US takes that future growth out of Europe and contributes to the persistent differences in productivity growth.

Europe has not adopted digital technologies to the same degree as the US

For most of the economy, productivity depends less on inventing technology than on putting it to work. The US pulled ahead after 1995 largely because its retailers, wholesalers and financial firms adopted information technology faster and used it better, and the sectors that digitised most grew fastest. About 50% of the US-EU productivity growth differences can be explained by differences in technology adoption (Gordon and Sayed, 2020; Bloom, Sadun and Van Reenen, 2012). 

How regulation impacts tech creation and adoption

Regulation affects technology creation and adoption, and with them productivity growth, in several ways. When regulation has a high fixed-cost component, for instance because it is hard to understand, requires up-front investment, or carries significant legal uncertainty, it creates barriers to market entry and technology adoption. As the chart below shows, when firms must devote resources to regulatory compliance, productivity falls and fewer new firms enter.
Regulation can also change the direction of technological change. Blind et al. (2024) show that the GDPR led to a substantial shift from radical to incremental product innovation, as compliance absorbed resources that firms would otherwise have used to develop entirely new products. The GDPR also weighed on entry and investment. Jia et al. (2021) find negative effects on European ventures relative to their US counterparts, in both the number of financing deals and the amounts raised, and these effects are particularly pronounced for newer, data-related and consumer-facing ventures. Similar patterns appear elsewhere. Rong et al. (2024) find that China’s regulation of large platforms reduced investment and entry in industries connected to the regulated platforms. Early evidence from Yang (2025) shows that the DMA negatively affected new entry and investment in DMA-related industries. 
Together, these barriers to entry and investment reduce economic dynamism. Less dynamism shields incumbents from competition and raises profits. Bessen (2016) documents this for the US: major expansions of regulation increase profits significantly. Conway and Nicoletti (2007) show how product market regulation helps explain why Europe lagged in the ICT era. Arnold et al. (2008) find that tight regulation of services slowed growth in ICT-using sectors, especially in continental Europe. Zona Mattioli and Borowiecki (2025) show that stricter regulation weakens the productivity of US subsidiaries in Europe. 
Regulation can also support technology creation and adoption. Rules that safeguard competition or build trust can widen markets. Aghion et al. (2005) find that competition spurs innovation, especially in industries where firms are close rivals, although the effect weakens at high levels of competition. Andrews et al. (2016) find that the divergence between frontier firms and laggards is stronger in industries where product market regulation is less competition-friendly, which underlines the importance of effective competition rules. Regulation can also build consumer trust. Rösner et al. (2020) find that the original Unfair Commercial Practices Directive, creating an EU-wide consumer protection floor, is associated with higher consumer trust and more cross-border purchases in countries with low consumer protection before its introduction. All of this shows that good regulatory design matters.

Europe spends more of its workforce on compliance than the US, and the share is rising

The EU body of law has not just grown in size, but also complexity. As documented by Hurka et al. (2022), laws have become structurally more complex, terminology and sentence structure have become less comprehensible, and the degree of interdependence among legal provisions within and across texts has increased. Fragmented enforcement and interaction with national legal systems add to the complexity. Unsurprisingly, European firms devote a higher share of their employment to compliance. To illustrate the rising burden, consider the case of Germany. According to its national employment agency, from 2022-2025 German firms had to hire an additional 325,000 compliance staff. The number one area of concern for companies: GDPR compliance. Recently, EU Court of Justice vice-president Thomas von Danwitz stated that tech legislation was “on the brink of being un-understandable.”

What firms say

Surveys support the view that regulatory complexity hinders EU tech creation and adoption. For instance, among EU firms that considered using AI in 2025, a lack of expertise was the most common barrier, but the next two were legal: unclear legal consequences and fear of breaching data-protection rules. Both have risen since 2024. 

The burden is not specific to technology: rules everywhere compete for the same staff and budgets that innovation and adoption need. 69% of EU firms say regulation limits their investment, and 64% of SMEs call administrative burden a persistent challenge.
