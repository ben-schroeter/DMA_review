Rules versus standards:
How prescriptive are EU tech rules?

Rules and standards represent two different mechanisms for legal and regulatory design. In his seminal article Rules Versus Standards: An Economic Analysis, Louis Kaplow argued that the choice between them turns on when the law is given content (in advance by the lawmaker, or after the fact by an adjudicator) and on the costs at each stage. A closely related question is whether regulation should operate ex ante or ex post. Ex-ante rules are preferable when the benefit of distinguishing exceptional cases is smaller than the extra cost of doing so, including the competitive cost of delay. Ex-post enforcement is preferable when case-specific evidence improves decisions enough to justify those costs. A third question is how complex and prescriptive the law should be. Cary Coglianese’s rule-design framework classifies each regulatory requirement along two axes. The first is means versus ends: does the requirement tell firms what to do, or what to achieve? The second is micro versus macro: is the requirement narrow and specific, or broad and general?

EU law is generally regarded as more prescriptive than US law. It has more rules, its rules tend to be more specific, and it places greater weight on ex-ante regulation. This reflects, at least in part, the EU’s commitment to the precautionary principle. In Coglianese’s terms, EU tech regulation leans toward means-based, micro-level requirements. That tendency has grown as EU tech law has shifted from Directives, which leave member states room to decide how to implement them, to directly applicable Regulations. Open-ended standards have increasingly given way to detailed rules designed to harmonize the law across the Union. Even competition law, long the domain of broad ex-post standards, has moved toward ex-ante rules for the digital economy, most visibly through the Digital Markets Act.

This section examines how far EU tech law relies on rules rather than standards, and how detailed those rules are. We also compare EU tech laws to their US counterparts and use the Sarbanes-Oxley Act as an external benchmark for prescriptiveness.

Three in four EU obligations are rules, with implementing and delegated acts adding further complexity

Of the 16,169 obligations in the 100 laws, 71% are rules; for obligations on companies the share is 71%. Implementing and delegated acts add 9,083 more obligations, of which 84% are rules, and in 24 of the 25 laws with a sizeable second layer they raise the share of rules.

How we coded. Every obligation counted on this site was read against one test, from Kaplow, "Rules Versus Standards: An Economic Analysis" (Duke Law Journal, 1992): can the addressee know from the text, before acting, what complies, without a later decision by an authority or court? Rule: a fixed action, ban, number, deadline, list or format. Standard: an open term such as appropriate, reasonable, proportionate, fair or effective. Mixed: a fixed duty with a substantive open element. Specified later: the provision itself says a delegated or implementing act, technical standard or guidelines will set its content. Left to member states: content chosen in transposition. The base is 16,169 obligations in the main acts and 9,083 in their implementing and delegated acts, after leaving out statements that impose no duty, on the same test as the grid below (2,230 and 1,333). On a hand-coded check of 200 obligations the coding agreed 92% of the time; it leans slightly towards "rule", so the shares of standards are about 5 points too low. Obligations on companies: those whose addressee is a company, its officers or its staff, coded as elsewhere on this site. Means or ends

A third of company obligations leave the means to the firm

Kaplow's test asks whether compliance is fixed in advance. Coglianese's grid asks a second question: does the law tell firms what to do, or what to achieve? Of the obligations on companies in the main laws, 65% prescribe the action itself. The rest leave the means to the firm: 7% set a specific result, 7% require a management system the firm designs, and 21% impose a general duty. Implementing and delegated acts are more prescriptive: only 24% of their obligations on companies leave the means open.

The share depends on the business model: from 21% for the obligations every company faces to 38% for makers of connected products, whose essential requirements are mostly general duties. Finance firms and cloud, data and AI providers face the most management-based duties, such as risk-management and governance systems. In finance the main laws leave the means open more often (38%) than the implementing and delegated acts that fill them in (26%).

How we coded. Every obligation was read a second time against Cary Coglianese's rule-design grid (Rule Design, 2024): means or ends, micro (specific) or macro (general). Prescriptive rule: a specific action, content, format or ban. Performance rule: a specific result, with the means left to the firm. Management-based duty: a plan, policy, procedure or risk assessment the firm designs. General duty: an open-ended result such as appropriate security or safety. Statements that impose no duty are left out (12% of items in the main laws); the rules-and-standards chart above uses the same base. On a pilot of 100 obligations the reader agreed with an independent reference coding in 89 cases (kappa 0.82); both readings were made by an AI model, and a hand check is still to come. Benchmarking EU digital acts against US laws

We counted the obligations in EU digital acts and in US laws the same way: first against US laws that do the same job, then against the Sarbanes-Oxley Act, a byword in the US for a demanding law.

In eight matched pairs, the EU act contains more legal obligations in seven and the same number in one: 2.0 times as many at the median, 286 against 111 on average. Counting only obligations that fall on companies, the EU act has more in six pairs, about the same in one and fewer in one (trade secrets): 2.0 times as many at the median, 114 against 33 on average. The widest gap is in AI: the AI Act has 764 obligations against 30 in Colorado's AI Act, the first comprehensive US AI law, and 346 against 29 on companies. Product safety follows, where US statutes leave most duties on manufacturers to agency rules. EU acts are also denser, with 13.7 obligations per 1,000 words against 9.1.

How we chose the pairs. We looked for the closest US counterpart to all 100 adopted laws and graded each match: 14 close, 41 partial, 8 agency rule only, 7 state law only, 2 proposed but never enacted, 12 with no counterpart. Many have no real equivalent: some are EU-specific by design (roaming, the .eu domain, budget programmes), and in other areas the US relies on general antitrust or case law. We measured the close matches, plus GDPR against California's privacy law and the AI Act against Colorado's AI Act, and kept the pairs that pass four tests: same job, same addressees, same kind of instrument (a law against a law) and comparable scope. How to read the numbers. Counts, not costs: an obligation is a rule, not a price tag. Against Sarbanes-Oxley, recent EU acts carry more obligations, and many more on companies

Counted the same way, recent EU digital acts carry more obligations than Sarbanes-Oxley. The gap widens when only obligations on companies are counted: Sarbanes-Oxley puts 59 on companies, against 346 in the AI Act, 185 in the Digital Services Act and 115 in the average EU digital act adopted since 2020. Much of what Sarbanes-Oxley asks of companies is spelled out in SEC rules, which are not counted here.
