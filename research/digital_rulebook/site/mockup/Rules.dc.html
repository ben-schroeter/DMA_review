The EU Digital Acquis: 110 laws,
22,800 obligations, 2.6m words of legal text

This page provides a quantitative analysis of EU tech law: its length, the number and type of legal obligations, who they bind, and how rule-making has developed over time. It is an extension of existing research on the growth of EU tech law (Marcus and Sekut, 2024) and builds on similar approaches developed for other jurisdictions (McLaughlin, 2017). The starting point is the mapping of EU tech law developed by Zenner and Marcus (2026).

More than half of today's digital laws date from 2020 or later

Regulations, which apply directly in every member state, now outnumber directives almost two to one. Since 2020 the new acts have spread across almost every policy area, led by industrial policy, research and connectivity. Implementing and delegated acts, which set the technical detail, have grown faster still: 542 of its 812 acts date from 2020 or later.

New acts carry more obligations, but not denser ones

The average act adopted since 2025 contains about 250 obligations, 2.5 times the average in the early 2000s (97). For comparison: the US Sarbanes-Oxley Act, a major piece of financial market regulation contains 240 obligations. The EU's acts are getting longer, not more tightly written: obligations per 1,000 words have stayed between 9.6 and 15.9 for two decades.

Laws grow in negotiation

The tendency of EU laws to get more complex during the legislative process is well-known. As far back as 1992, Jacques Delors, then President of the Commission, famously stated: "We must be inventors of simplicity which must lead to a collective examination of conscience, firstly within the Commission, for whom the pen must be lighter and the texts plainer (...); the quest for compromise at Council level results in texts which are too complicated, even incomprehensible."

Indeed, in 84 of 104 cases the act that came out of Parliament and Council had more obligations than the Commission's proposal. The median act grew by 44% in words and 26% in obligations. The largest increases: the AI Act (392 to 764), MiCA (766 to 1,114) and the European Health Data Space (395 to 603).

Rules aimed directly at companies have quadrupled since 2018

About 18,400 obligations name a private party, such as an undertaking, provider, platform or controller, as the one who must act: 9,553 in the main acts and 8,867 in their implementing and delegated acts. The average act adopted since 2020 binds companies directly 113 times, against 39 in the 2000s.

A lower bound Many obligations formally addressed to Member States or EU bodies reach companies too: directives that say "Member States shall ensure that entities…", duties to create enforcers with fining powers, Commission designations that switch obligations on, and supervisory fees. Checked against a fresh hand-coded sample, our addressee coding has a 90% accuracy rate. Counting duties on Member States whose content is company conduct (“Member States shall ensure that providers…”) would add about 540 more obligations.

Obligations by policy area

Rules that tell companies what to do have grown in every policy area since the start of 2018: from ×1.4 in IP to ×9 in e-commerce and consumer protection. Cybersecurity, industrial policy and research & innovation had no such obligations before 2018. Finance and Trust & Safety now hold about 3,900 and 8,600. In Finance, more than half sit in implementing and delegated acts rather than in the laws themselves.

Newer laws tell companies what to do, not what not to do

In laws adopted since 2018, prohibitions make up 6.2% of obligations, down from about 7.4% in older laws. The newer laws lean on positive duties instead: assess, document, report, ensure.

One in six obligations is a duty to report, notify, disclose or keep records

6,263 of the obligations in adopted EU digital laws and their implementing and delegated acts require someone to pass on, publish, register or keep information. Companies carry 2,630 of them, member states 2,216 and EU institutions 1,416. For companies the number has grown 3.6-fold since the start of 2018, from 726. The Markets in Crypto-Assets Regulation alone accounts for 331, followed by the Medical Devices Regulation (191) and the In Vitro Diagnostic Medical Devices Regulation (176).

Companies’ reporting duties have grown in every policy area

A reporting obligation is a counted obligation whose required action is to report, notify, submit or provide information, publish or disclose it, register, or keep records. Not included: rules that only set a report’s content or format, bans on disclosure, and reporting inside a company to its own management. Counted automatically: in hand-checked samples not used to build the rules, 94% of counted items were genuine reporting obligations, and the count captures an estimated 83% of them, so 6,263 is a lower bound (likely about 6,200).
