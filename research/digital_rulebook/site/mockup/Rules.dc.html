The EU Digital Acquis: 110 laws,
22,800 obligations, 2.6m words of legal text

This page provides a quantitative overview of EU tech laws. The focus is on instruments that regulate digital technologies, services, data and infrastructure, or set key rules for digital markets; funding programmes, industrial and crisis instruments, and rules on public bodies’ internal workings are excluded. It is an extension of existing research on the growth of EU tech law (Xanthaki, 2025) and builds on similar approaches developed for other jurisdictions (McLaughlin, 2017). The starting point is the mapping of EU tech law developed by Zenner and Marcus (2026), but adapted to the narrower focus of this project.

Almost half of today's obligations date from 2020 or later

Regulations, which apply directly in every member state, now slightly outnumber directives. Since 2020 the new acts have spread across almost every policy area, led by data, AI and cybersecurity (8 acts) and finance (8). Implementing and delegated acts, which set the technical detail, have grown faster still: 536 of its 803 acts date from 2020 or later.

New acts carry more obligations, but not denser ones

The average act adopted since 2025 contains about 250 obligations, 2.5 times the average in the early 2000s (97). For comparison: the US Sarbanes-Oxley Act, a major piece of financial market regulation contains 240 obligations. The EU's acts are getting longer, not denser: obligations per 1,000 words have stayed between 9.6 and 15.9 for two decades.

Laws grow in negotiation

The tendency of EU laws to get more complex during the legislative process is well-known. As far back as 1992, Jacques Delors, then President of the Commission, famously stated: "We must be inventors of simplicity which must lead to a collective examination of conscience, firstly within the Commission, for whom the pen must be lighter and the texts plainer (...); the quest for compromise at Council level results in texts which are too complicated, even incomprehensible."

Indeed, in 84 of 104 cases the act that came out of Parliament and Council had more obligations than the Commission's proposal. The median act grew by 44% in words and 26% in obligations. The largest increases: the Medical Devices Regulation (923 to 1,571), the In Vitro Diagnostic Medical Devices Regulation (916 to 1,427), the AI Act (392 to 764) and MiCA (766 to 1,114).

Rules aimed directly at companies have nearly quadrupled since 2018

About 18,900 obligations name a private party, such as an undertaking, provider, platform or controller, as the one who must act: 10,147 in the main acts and 8,764 in their implementing and delegated acts. The average act adopted since 2020 binds companies directly 118 times, against 43 in the 2000s.

A lower bound Many obligations formally addressed to Member States or EU bodies reach companies too: directives that say "Member States shall ensure that entities…", duties to create enforcers with fining powers, Commission designations that switch obligations on, and supervisory fees. Checked against a fresh hand-coded sample, our addressee coding has a 90% accuracy rate. Counting duties on Member States whose content is company conduct (“Member States shall ensure that providers…”) would add about 550 more obligations.

Obligations by policy area

Rules that tell companies what to do have grown in every policy area since the start of 2018: from ×1.3 in IP to ×13 in AI and digital trust. Cybersecurity had no such obligations before 2018. Product safety and Finance now hold the most: about 7,600 and 4,200. 67% of the product-safety total comes from just three laws, the Vehicle General Safety Regulation, the Medical Devices Regulation and the in vitro diagnostics regulation. In Finance, more than half sit in implementing and delegated acts rather than in the laws themselves.

Newer laws tell companies what to do, not what not to do

In laws adopted since 2018, prohibitions make up 6.2% of obligations, down from 11.1% in laws adopted before 2010. The newer laws lean on positive duties instead: assess, document, report, ensure.

One in six obligations is a duty to report, notify, disclose or keep records

6,263 of the obligations in adopted EU digital laws and their implementing and delegated acts require someone to pass on, publish, register or keep information. Companies carry 2,630 of them, member states 2,216 and EU institutions 1,416. For companies the number has grown 3.6-fold since the start of 2018, from 726.

Companies’ reporting duties have grown in every policy area

A reporting obligation is a counted obligation whose required action is to report, notify, submit or provide information, publish or disclose it, register, or keep records. Not included: rules that only set a report’s content or format, bans on disclosure, and reporting inside a company to its own management. Counted automatically: in hand-checked samples not used to build the rules, 94% of counted items were genuine reporting obligations, and the count captures an estimated 83% of them, so 6,263 is a lower bound (likely about 7,100).
