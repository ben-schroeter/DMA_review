# How EU digital rules compare with US law

*Draft for the website: the Findings page (Rules) with a link from the Methodology page.*

## The short answer

We compared ten EU digital acts with the US laws that do the same job. In every pair, the EU act contains more legal restrictions: rules that say someone *shall*, *must*, *shall not* or *may only* do something. The typical EU act has about 2.4 times as many as its US counterpart. On average that is 215 restrictions against 102. The EU acts are also denser: a median of 14.1 restrictions per 1,000 words against 8.8. Length alone doesn't explain the gap.

## Why compare with the US at all?

A count of restrictions means little on its own. Is 200 a lot for a law? The United States is the obvious yardstick. It is an advanced economy with large digital firms, and it regulates many of the same problems: product safety, privacy, trade secrets, government data, chip manufacturing. If both sides regulate the same thing, a comparison of how many rules each writes says something about how each legislates.

## How we chose the pairs

We started from all 115 instruments in the dataset and looked for the closest US counterpart to each. We graded every match:

- **Close:** a US law with the same core purpose and similar scope (16 of 115)
- **Partial:** overlapping, but narrower, broader or working differently (64)
- **Other:** only a state law, only an agency rule, only a bill that never passed, or no counterpart at all (35)

Many EU instruments have no real US equivalent. Some are EU-specific by design, such as mobile roaming rules, the .eu domain and budget programmes. In other areas the US relies on general antitrust law or case law instead of a dedicated statute. The full mapping is published with the data.

We measured only the close matches, plus GDPR against California's privacy law, since there is no federal one. We then reviewed each pair for fairness and kept the ten where the comparison holds. A pair counts as fair when:

1. **Same job:** both laws aim at the same problem.
2. **Same addressees:** both bind the same kind of actor, such as companies, public bodies or courts.
3. **Same kind of instrument:** a law against a law, not a law against a detailed agency rulebook.
4. **Comparable scope:** neither covers large areas the other doesn't.

## The ten pairs

| Chips Act (2023) | CHIPS Act (2022) | Both aim to build domestic chip manufacturing. | 242 / 95 |

| Trade Secrets Directive (2016) | Defend Trade Secrets Act (2016) | Both give firms civil remedies against theft of trade secrets. Adopted within weeks of each other. | 60 / 33 |

### Caveats on three pairs

- **GDPR and the CCPA:** the CCPA is a state law, narrower in scope, and much of its detail sits in regulations from California's privacy agency.
- **Toy safety:** the US act covers all children's products, not just toys. The detailed toy standard is set by an agency rule adopting an industry standard.
- **Unfair practices:** section 5 is a single broad prohibition. Most of its content comes from court rulings and Federal Trade Commission rules, which a word count can't see.

### What explains the gap

Two patterns recur. First, EU acts add obligations on member states, such as designating authorities, reporting and cooperating, that a federal US law doesn't need. Second, several EU acts build standing regulatory machinery alongside their main purpose. The Chips Act is an example. The US CHIPS Act is mainly funding, a tax credit and conditions attached to that money. The EU act adds a crisis regime: it can oblige companies to supply information, make designated factories accept priority orders, and organise joint purchasing.

## Pairs we left out, and why

We measured seven further pairs but excluded them because they would distort the result:

- **Copyright (InfoSoc) and the DMCA:** the DMCA also covers online safe harbours and other topics, so it is much broader.
- **EU merger control and the Hart-Scott-Rodino Act:** HSR covers only the filing procedure. The substantive test sits elsewhere in US law.
- **Anti-money laundering:** the US comparator would be a short 1970 law against a comprehensive 2024 EU rulebook.
- **Electromagnetic compatibility:** the US counterpart is a detailed technical rulebook of the Federal Communications Commission, not a law.
- **Financial data access (FiDA):** still an EU proposal, set against a US statute plus an agency rule.
- **Non-cash payment fraud:** the EU directive mainly harmonises national criminal law, with duties for member states that federal law doesn't need.
- **Toy Safety Regulation (2025):** it would count the same US act twice. We kept the 2009 Directive, which is in force and of the same era as the US act. The 2025 Regulation replaces it from 2030.

## How to read the numbers

- **Counts, not costs:** a restriction is a rule, not a price tag. One obligation can be trivial and another expensive. The comparison shows how much each system writes into law, not what complying costs.
- **US law delegates more to agencies:** many US statutes leave the detail to agency rules, and EU acts are often followed by implementing and delegated acts. We compare law with law and leave both kinds of secondary rule out. Adding them would raise both sides, and the gap could narrow.
- **Approximate counts:** restrictions are counted automatically. Hand-checks found 84% of the counted items in EU acts and 80% in US laws were genuine restrictions. That is close enough for comparisons of this size, but not for differences of a few rules.
- **Which text was measured:** EU figures use the original act's articles and annexes. US figures use the enacted law, including the text it inserted into the US Code, since that inserted text is the substantive rule. For section 5 of the FTC Act and the California act we used the current text.

The full method, every pair's figures and the excluded pairs are in the data download and on the Methodology page.
