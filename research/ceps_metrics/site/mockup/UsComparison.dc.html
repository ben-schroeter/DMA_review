Findings · EU vs US

EU digital acts against US laws

We counted the obligations in EU digital acts and in US laws the same way: first against US laws that do the same job, then against the Sarbanes-Oxley Act, a byword in the US for a demanding law.

In eight matched pairs, the EU act contains more legal obligations in seven and the same number in one: 2.0 times as many at the median, 286 against 111 on average. Counting only obligations that fall on companies, the EU act has more in six pairs, about the same in one and fewer in one (trade secrets): 2.0 times as many at the median, 114 against 33 on average. The widest gap is in AI: the AI Act has 764 obligations against 30 in Colorado's AI Act, the first comprehensive US AI law, and 346 against 29 on companies. Product safety follows, where US statutes leave most duties on manufacturers to agency rules. EU acts are also denser, with 13.7 obligations per 1,000 words against 9.1.

How we chose the pairs. We looked for the closest US counterpart to all 115 instruments and graded each match: 16 close, 64 partial, 9 agency rule only, 8 state law only, 2 proposed but never enacted, 16 with no counterpart. Many have no real equivalent: some are EU-specific by design (roaming, the .eu domain, budget programmes), and in other areas the US relies on general antitrust or case law. We measured the close matches, plus GDPR against California's privacy law and the AI Act against Colorado's AI Act, and kept the pairs that pass four tests: same job, same addressees, same kind of instrument (a law against a law) and comparable scope. How to read the numbers. Counts, not costs: an obligation is a rule, not a price tag. Law against law: US agency rules and EU implementing acts are left out on both sides; adding them could narrow the gap. Approximate: 89% of counted items in EU acts and 80% in US laws were genuine obligations. US figures include text inserted into the US Code, since that is the substantive rule. Obligations on companies. Obligations whose addressee is a private company, its officers or its staff. EU counts use the same coding as the rest of this site; every US obligation the automatic coding left unclear was read by hand. Three pairs in which both laws mainly bind public bodies (data protection and cybersecurity in the EU institutions, open government data) are not shown. Colorado's AI Act (2024) is a state law, matched because there is no federal AI law; it covers only high-risk AI used in decisions about consumers, while the AI Act also bans some practices and regulates general-purpose models, so part of that gap is scope. Rules or standards? Three in four EU obligations are rules, and the second layer adds more

Louis Kaplow distinguishes rules, whose content is fixed before people act ("notify within 72 hours"), from standards, whose content is decided afterwards by a regulator or court ("take appropriate measures"). Read obligation by obligation, 73% of those in the 105 EU digital laws are rules and 25% are standards. Obligations on companies look much the same: 72% rules. In the implementing and delegated acts that follow, 84% are rules, and 83% of those on companies: in 26 of the 30 laws with a sizeable second layer, the Commission's follow-up acts turn open terms into fixed ones. For companies the shift is largest in the Ecodesign Regulation (65% to 94%), the Cybersecurity Act (62% to 88%) and DORA (55% to 81%). NIS2 is the main exception: its implementing rules on security measures keep the "appropriate measures" style, and the share of rules for companies falls from 75% to 52%.

How we coded. Every obligation counted on this site was read against one test, from Kaplow, "Rules Versus Standards: An Economic Analysis" (Duke Law Journal, 1992): can the addressee know from the text, before acting, what complies, without a later decision by an authority or court? Rule: a fixed action, ban, number, deadline, list or format. Standard: an open term such as appropriate, reasonable, proportionate, fair or effective. Mixed: a fixed duty with a substantive open element. Specified later: the provision itself says a delegated or implementing act, technical standard or guidelines will set its content. Left to member states: content chosen in transposition. The base is 20,212 obligations in the main acts and 10,544 in their implementing and delegated acts, after leaving out statements that impose no duty (1,137 and 833). On a hand-coded check of 200 obligations the coding agreed 92% of the time; it leans slightly towards "rule", so the shares of standards are about 5 points too low. Obligations on companies: those whose addressee is a company, its officers or its staff, coded as elsewhere on this site. The lower chart shows laws with at least 60 obligations in their implementing and delegated acts and at least 50 in the main act.

Against Sarbanes-Oxley, recent EU acts carry more obligations, and many more on companies

Counted the same way, recent EU digital acts carry more obligations than Sarbanes-Oxley. The gap widens when only obligations on companies are counted: Sarbanes-Oxley puts 59 on companies, against 346 in the AI Act, 185 in the Digital Services Act and 89 in the average EU digital act adopted since 2020. Much of what Sarbanes-Oxley asks of companies is spelled out in SEC rules, which are not counted here.

Sarbanes-Oxley Act 2002, enacted text; EU acts: original adopted texts, articles and annexes. Counter precision: 89% on EU acts, 68% on Sarbanes-Oxley. Obligations on companies: those addressed to a company, its officers or its staff; every Sarbanes-Oxley obligation was read by hand.
