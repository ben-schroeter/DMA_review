Findings · EU vs US

EU digital acts against US laws

We counted the obligations in EU digital acts and in US laws the same way: first against US laws that do the same job, then against the Sarbanes-Oxley Act, a byword in the US for a demanding law.

Matched against US laws doing the same job, EU acts carry twice the rules, and twice those on companies

In eight matched pairs, the EU act contains more legal obligations in seven and the same number in one: 2.0 times as many at the median, 286 against 111 on average. Counting only obligations that fall on companies, the EU act has more in six pairs, about the same in one and fewer in one (trade secrets): 2.0 times as many at the median, 114 against 33 on average. The widest gap is in AI: the AI Act has 764 obligations against 30 in Colorado's AI Act, the first comprehensive US AI law, and 346 against 29 on companies. Product safety follows, where US statutes leave most duties on manufacturers to agency rules. EU acts are also denser, with 13.7 obligations per 1,000 words against 9.1.

How we chose the pairs. We looked for the closest US counterpart to all 115 instruments and graded each match: 16 close, 64 partial, 9 agency rule only, 8 state law only, 2 proposed but never enacted, 16 with no counterpart. Many have no real equivalent: some are EU-specific by design (roaming, the .eu domain, budget programmes), and in other areas the US relies on general antitrust or case law. We measured the close matches, plus GDPR against California's privacy law and the AI Act against Colorado's AI Act, and kept the pairs that pass four tests: same job, same addressees, same kind of instrument (a law against a law) and comparable scope. How to read the numbers. Counts, not costs: an obligation is a rule, not a price tag. Law against law: US agency rules and EU implementing acts are left out on both sides; adding them could narrow the gap. Approximate: 89% of counted items in EU acts and 80% in US laws were genuine obligations. US figures include text inserted into the US Code, since that is the substantive rule. Obligations on companies. Obligations whose addressee is a private company, its officers or its staff. EU counts use the same coding as the rest of this site; every US obligation the automatic coding left unclear was read by hand. Three pairs in which both laws mainly bind public bodies (data protection and cybersecurity in the EU institutions, open government data) are not shown. Colorado's AI Act (2024) is a state law, matched because there is no federal AI law; it covers only high-risk AI used in decisions about consumers, while the AI Act also bans some practices and regulates general-purpose models, so part of that gap is scope. The full mapping of all 115 instruments is in the data download.

Against Sarbanes-Oxley, recent EU acts carry more obligations, and many more on companies

Counted the same way, recent EU digital acts carry more obligations than Sarbanes-Oxley and pack them more densely. The gap widens when only obligations on companies are counted: Sarbanes-Oxley puts 59 on companies, against 346 in the AI Act, 185 in the Digital Services Act and 89 in the average EU digital act adopted since 2020. Much of what Sarbanes-Oxley asks of companies is spelled out in SEC rules, which are not counted here.

Sarbanes-Oxley Act 2002, enacted text; EU acts: original adopted texts, articles and annexes. Counter precision: 89% on EU acts, 68% on Sarbanes-Oxley. Obligations on companies: those addressed to a company, its officers or its staff; every Sarbanes-Oxley obligation was read by hand.
