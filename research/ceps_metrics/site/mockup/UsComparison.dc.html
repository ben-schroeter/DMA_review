EU digital acts against the Sarbanes-Oxley Act

Sarbanes-Oxley is a byword in the US for a demanding law. Counted the same way, recent EU digital acts carry more obligations and pack them more densely.

Sarbanes-Oxley Act 2002, enacted text; EU acts: original adopted texts, articles and annexes. Counter precision: 89% on EU acts, 68% on Sarbanes-Oxley.

Matched against US laws doing the same job, EU acts carry about twice the rules

In ten matched pairs, the EU act contains more legal obligations in nine and the same number in one: 2.3 times as many at the median, 212 against 102 on average. EU acts are also denser, with 13.9 obligations per 1,000 words against 8.8, so length alone does not explain the gap.

How we chose the pairs

We looked for the closest US counterpart to all 115 instruments and graded each match. Many have no real equivalent: some are EU-specific by design (roaming, the .eu domain, budget programmes), and in other areas the US relies on general antitrust or case law.

We measured only the close matches, plus GDPR against California's privacy law, then kept the pairs that pass four tests: same job, same addressees, same kind of instrument (a law against a law), and comparable scope.

Pairs we left out

  • InfoSoc vs DMCA: the DMCA is much broader.
  • Merger Regulation vs Hart-Scott-Rodino: HSR covers only the filing procedure.
  • Anti-money laundering: a 1970 enabling law against a 2024 rulebook.
  • Electromagnetic compatibility: the US counterpart is an FCC rulebook.
  • FiDA: a pending proposal against a statute plus a rule.
  • Non-cash payment fraud: the directive adds member-state duties.
  • Toy Safety Regulation 2025: would count the same US act twice.

How to read the numbers

  • Counts, not costs: an obligation is a rule, not a price tag.
  • Law against law: US agency rules and EU implementing acts are left out on both sides; adding them could narrow the gap.
  • Approximate: 89% of counted items in EU acts and 80% in US laws were genuine obligations.
  • US figures include text inserted into the US Code, since that is the substantive rule.
