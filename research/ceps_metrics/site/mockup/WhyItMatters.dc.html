About · Why it matters

Europe's growth problem is largely a technology problem

Europe caught up with American productivity in the half-century after the war, then fell behind as the US pulled ahead in information technology. How Europe regulates technology is one of the explanations on the table. This page sets out the diagnosis, what economic research says about regulation and growth, and the counter-arguments.

Europeans have less income per head than Americans mainly because each hour of work produces less, not because they work fewer hours: about 72% of the 2023 gap comes from lower output per hour. That productivity gap opened after 1995, just as the US began to gain from information technology.

Europe has not grown its own tech giants

Count the public companies founded from scratch in the last 50 years that are now worth more than $10 billion. The US has 241, together worth about $29.6 trillion; seven of them, led by Apple, Nvidia and Microsoft, are each worth a trillion dollars or more. The EU has 14, worth $0.43 trillion in total, about one-seventieth as much. Its largest, Spotify, is worth about $90 billion. Half of the EU's value is in tech firms; the rest spans biotech, logistics, private equity, aviation, jewellery and real estate.

The mechanism

Regulation doesn't stop Europe from inventing technology. It stops Europe from using it.

For most of the economy, productivity depends less on inventing technology than on putting it to work. The US pulled ahead after 1995 largely because its retailers, wholesalers and financial firms adopted information technology faster and used it better, and the sectors that digitised most grew fastest.

Two OECD studies, nearly two decades apart, reach the same conclusion. Conway and Nicoletti (2007) show why Europe missed the ICT productivity boom. Countries that kept rigid rules into the late 1990s, mostly in continental Europe, met the internet era with sheltered incumbents. Those firms invested less in ICT, used it less well, and fell further behind the frontier with every leap in technology. The damage hit hardest in services, the sectors that bought most from heavily regulated industries.

Zona Mattioli and Borowiecki (2025) show the problem persists. Tracking EU subsidiaries of US firms from 2000 to 2021, they find productivity gains at the American parent reach European subsidiaries roughly 40% less in the most rigid EU economies than in the least rigid. In those economies, workers are also slower to move to the firms that use technology best. The lesson for the AI era is plain: the more rigid the rulebook, the faster each new technology leaves you behind. Measuring that rulebook is the first step to fixing it.

What firms say

Surveys point the same way. Among EU firms that considered using AI in 2025, a lack of expertise was the most common barrier, but the next two were legal: unclear legal consequences and fear of breaching data-protection rules. Both have risen since 2024. Eurobarometer 578 finds legal uncertainty holding back AI adoption for 49%.

The burden is not specific to technology, which is part of the point: rules everywhere compete for the same staff and budgets that adoption needs. 69% of EU firms say regulation limits their investment, 64% of SMEs call administrative burden a persistent challenge, and red tape made German firms hire about 325,000 extra staff in three years.

Each link in this chain has evidence behind it; no single study has yet estimated the whole path from EU digital rules to the aggregate productivity gap. Survey figures are self-reported, and several cover all regulation, not digital rules alone.

In Draghi's words

“Regulatory barriers to scaling up are particularly onerous in the tech sector, especially for young companies (...). Regulatory barriers constrain growth in several ways. First, complex and costly procedures across fragmented national systems discourage inventors from filing Intellectual Property Rights (IPRs), hindering young companies from leveraging the Single Market. Second, the EU’s regulatory stance towards tech companies hampers innovation: the EU now has around 100 tech-focused laws and over 270 regulators active in digital networks across all Member States. Many EU laws take a precautionary approach, dictating specific business practices ex ante to avert potential risks ex post. For example, the AI Act imposes additional regulatory requirements on general purpose AI models that exceed a pre-defined threshold of computational power – a threshold which some state-of-the-art models already exceed.

Third, digital companies are deterred from doing business across the EU via subsidiaries, as they face heterogeneous requirements, a proliferation of regulatory agencies and “gold plating” of EU legislation by national authorities. Fourth, limitations on data storing and processing create high compliance costs and hinder the creation of large, integrated data sets for training AI models. This fragmentation puts EU companies at a disadvantage relative to the US, which relies on the private sector to build vast data sets, and China, which can leverage its central institutions for data aggregation. (...) The net effect of this burden of regulation is that only larger companies – which are often non-EU based – have the financial capacity and incentive to bear the costs of complying. Young innovative tech companies may choose not to operate in the EU at all.”

Mario Draghi, The future of European competitiveness (2024)

The volume of rules

Brussels legislates at a pace Washington does not

Overlaps add cost: a BusinessEurope review of 13 EU laws found 169 issues regulated by more than one of them, with differing or inconsistent requirements in many cases. More than 60% of EU firms call regulation an obstacle to investment.

This project's dataset puts a number on the digital part: 115 instruments containing about 24,300 legal obligations, with another 10,800 in their implementing and delegated acts.

What the research says

Design matters more than volume

Across 57 studies, heavy or poorly designed regulation tends to weigh hardest on small firms, protect incumbents, deter entry and slow the adoption of new technology. The evidence is strongest for entry barriers, size thresholds and regulatory uncertainty.

Market power and rents

Since 2000, regulation and lobbying explain a large share of rising US profits (Bessen 2016). Regulatory costs hit small firms and favour large ones (Singla 2023), which can afford whole policy teams (Hall & Sun 2026). The Commission finds EU markups up 7% since 2000, a possible 5.7% GDP cost.

Firms and jobs

France's 50-employee rules act like a 2.3% labour tax, cost 3.4% of GDP and cut innovation by 5.7% (Garicano et al.; Aghion et al.). Small firms pay up to 10× more per employee to comply (Commission, 2007).

Innovation

After GDPR, a third of apps exited and new app entry halved (Janssen et al.); EU venture deals fell relative to the US (Jia et al.). But well-designed rules can spur innovation, and platform "kill zones" justify intervention.

Productivity

Anticompetitive rules in upstream services slow productivity growth downstream (Bourlès et al.). Intra-EU barriers equal a 44% tariff on goods and 110% on services (IMF), while the single market adds 3–7% to GDP (Mayer et al.).

The other side

Is regulation really the cause?

Anu Bradford argues that Europe's tech gap owes more to its fragmented digital single market, shallow capital markets, bankruptcy law, risk aversion and talent constraints than to strict digital rules. Gutiérrez and Philippon find EU product markets have become more competitive than American ones, thanks to independent EU competition enforcement. In the US, Goldschlag and Tabarrok find rising federal regulation does not explain falling start-up rates. And EU rules that replace 27 national regimes can lower costs even as they add obligations.

The firm-level evidence on the GDPR is fairly consistent. The claim that EU digital rules explain the overall productivity gap is argued, not proven. This site's data is a step towards testing it.
