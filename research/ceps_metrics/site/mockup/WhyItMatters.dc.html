Why it matters

Europe's growth problem is largely a technology problem

Europe caught up with American productivity in the half-century after the war, then fell behind as the US pulled ahead in information technology. How Europe regulates technology is one of the explanations on the table. This page sets out the diagnosis, what economic research says about regulation and growth, and the counter-arguments.

The volume of rules

Brussels legislates at a pace Washington does not

The Draghi report counts around 100 tech-focused EU laws and more than 270 regulators active in digital networks across member states. Overlaps add cost: a BusinessEurope review of 13 EU laws found 169 issues regulated by more than one of them, with differing or inconsistent requirements in many cases. More than 60% of EU firms call regulation an obstacle to investment.

This project's dataset puts a number on the digital part: 115 instruments containing about 24,300 legal obligations, with another 10,800 in their implementing and delegated acts.

The mechanism

Growth comes from adopting technology, and regulation makes adoption harder

For most of the economy, productivity depends less on inventing technology than on putting it to work. The US pulled ahead after 1995 largely because its retailers, wholesalers and financial firms adopted information technology faster and used it better. Adoption is hardest for small firms: they have the least slack to absorb new systems, and every extra layer of red tape or legal uncertainty about how data, software or AI may be used raises the cost and risk of trying. Large firms can hire teams to manage that; small firms often wait.

1 · Adoption, not invention, drove US gains

Much of the 1995–2005 US productivity revival came from industries that use ICT intensively, such as retail, wholesale and finance. Europe fell behind through "a paucity of ICT investment" and "a failure to capture the efficiency benefits of ICT".

Gordon & Sayed (2020), NBER w27425 / VoxEU
2 · Adoption and firm growth close the gap

Within the EU, sectors and countries closer to US productivity are those where firms adopt technology, grow larger and enter and exit freely. The JRC's advice: improve technology adoption and remove barriers to firm growth and entry.

Martinez Turegano (2020), JRC122059
3 · Data rules raise the cost of data

After the GDPR, EU firms stored 26% less data and did 15% less computation than comparable US firms, like a 22% rise in the cost of data. A third of apps left the market.

Demirer et al. (2024); Janssen et al. (2022)
4 · Compliance absorbs people and time

68% of German firms name the GDPR as their most burdensome rule. Red tape overall made them hire about 325,000 extra staff in three years; 55% report lost productivity, 16% blocked innovation.

Diegmann & Kubis (2025), IAB survey of 9,209 establishments
5 · Small firms bear more, especially under uncertainty

Big companies run policy teams to track and shape rules: about 52 people at the average Fortune 100 firm, against one or two at the average firm. Small firms pay up to 10× more per employee to comply, and unclear rules delay pioneers: first-of-a-kind medical devices spend 34% longer in approval.

Hall & Sun (2026); European Commission (2007); Stern (2017)

Each link in this chain has evidence behind it; no single study has yet estimated the whole path from EU digital rules to the aggregate productivity gap. The IAB figures are self-reported and cover all bureaucracy, not the GDPR alone.

What the research says

Design matters more than volume

Across 57 studies, heavy or poorly designed regulation tends to weigh hardest on small firms, protect incumbents, deter entry and slow the adoption of new technology. The evidence is strongest for entry barriers, size thresholds and regulatory uncertainty.

Market power and rents

Since 2000, regulation and lobbying explain a large share of rising US profits (Bessen 2016). Regulatory costs hit small firms and favour large ones (Singla 2023), which can afford whole policy teams (Hall & Sun 2026). The Commission finds EU markups up 7% since 2000, a possible 5.7% GDP cost.

Firms and jobs

France's 50-employee rules act like a 2.3% labour tax, cost 3.4% of GDP and cut innovation by 5.7% (Garicano et al.; Aghion et al.). Small firms pay up to 10× more per employee to comply (Commission, 2007).

Innovation

After GDPR, a third of apps exited and new app entry halved (Janssen et al.); EU venture deals fell relative to the US (Jia et al.). But well-designed rules can spur innovation, and platform "kill zones" justify intervention.

Productivity

Anticompetitive rules in upstream services slow productivity growth downstream (Bourlès et al.). Intra-EU barriers equal a 44% tariff on goods and 110% on services (IMF), while the single market adds 3–7% to GDP (Mayer et al.).

The other side

Is regulation really the cause?

Anu Bradford argues that Europe's tech gap owes more to its fragmented digital single market, shallow capital markets, bankruptcy law, risk aversion and talent constraints than to strict digital rules. Gutiérrez and Philippon find EU product markets have become more competitive than American ones, thanks to independent EU competition enforcement. In the US, Goldschlag and Tabarrok find rising federal regulation does not explain falling start-up rates. And EU rules that replace 27 national regimes can lower costs even as they add obligations.

The firm-level evidence on the GDPR is fairly consistent. The claim that EU digital rules explain the overall productivity gap is argued, not proven. This site's data is a step towards testing it.
