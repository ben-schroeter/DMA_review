# What economics says about regulation and growth

*Draft for /why-it-matters/ (literature section). 57 studies; references with links in `data/literature/regulation_economics.json`. "(summary only)" marks figures checked only against a secondary source.*

## The short version

Regulation can correct real market failures, and well-designed rules sometimes spur innovation. But a large body of evidence finds that heavy or poorly designed regulation tends to:
- weigh hardest on small and young firms
- protect incumbents and raise market power
- deter entry and investment
- slow the adoption of new technology

Those are the channels through which the productivity gap with the US opened. The evidence is strongest for entry barriers and size-dependent rules. It is weaker and more contested for the claim that regulation explains Europe's overall tech gap.

## 1. Theory: who regulation serves

- **Capture** (Stigler 1971): regulation is often "acquired by the industry and designed and operated primarily for its benefit", because concentrated producers outbid dispersed consumers.
- **Rent-seeking** (Krueger 1974): rents created by restrictions invite wasteful competition for them. She estimated licence rents at about 7% of national income in India and 15% of GNP in Turkey (summary only).
- **Competition and innovation** (Aghion et al. 2005): innovation rises with competition up to a point, then falls (an inverted U), most steeply where firms are close rivals. Rules that blunt competition can therefore cut innovation.
- **Deregulation dynamics** (Blanchard & Giavazzi 2003): removing rents pays off in the long run but can hurt incumbents and workers in the short run, which is why reforms are politically hard.

## 2. Regulation, profits and market power

- **Bessen (2016), *Accounting for Rising Corporate Profits: Intangibles or Regulatory Rents?*:** R&D and capital explain much of the 1990s rise in US corporate profits, but since 2000 political activity and regulation account for "a surprisingly large share". Firms going through major regulatory change see valuations rise about 12% relative to matched firms (summary only).
- **Singla (2023):** US regulatory costs rose by about $1 trillion over 1970–2018. Higher costs cut sales, jobs and margins at small firms and raised them at large ones, explaining 31–37% of the rise in market power.
- **Gutiérrez & Philippon (2019):** since the late 1990s, US entry no longer responds to profitable opportunities. They blame lobbying and regulation, not technology.
- **Covarrubias, Gutiérrez & Philippon (2020):** concentration was "good" in the 1990s, driven by efficiency, and "bad" after 2000, with higher prices, lower investment and rising barriers.
- **De Loecker, Eeckhout & Unger (2020):** US markups rose from 21% to 61% above cost (1980–2016), driven by the top firms.
- **Europe is different:**
  - Philippon (2019) and Gutiérrez & Philippon (2023) find EU markets became *more* competitive than US ones, with lower concentration, lower excess profits and lower regulatory entry barriers. They credit independent EU competition enforcement.
  - **European Commission, *Protecting competition in a changing world* (2024):** across 23 European countries, average markups rose about 7% between 2000 and 2019 and industry concentration rose about 5 points. A model simulation attributes a GDP loss of about 5.7% to the markup rise if it fully reflects weaker competition.
  - Koltay, Lőrincz & Valletti (2023) find the same moderate rise. OECD work (Calligaris et al. 2018) finds markups highest and rising fastest in digital-intensive sectors.

- **Hall & Sun (2026):** large firms employ whole policy teams to track and shape regulation, very roughly 13 times the size of their lobbying teams. The average Fortune 100 firm has about 52 policy staff and 18 lobbyists; the average firm has 1.5 and 0.7. This is a scale advantage small firms and start-ups cannot match.

**Why it matters here:** much of the EU's digital rulebook (the DMA, P2B, the Data Act) aims at platform market power. The rents literature warns that complex rules can also entrench large incumbents, who absorb compliance costs more easily.

## 3. Firms: size, entry, compliance costs and jobs

- **Size thresholds:**
  - Garicano, Lelarge & Van Reenen (2016): France's rules that start at 50 employees work like a 2.3% tax on labour and cost 3.4% of GDP; firms bunch just below the threshold.
  - Aghion, Bergeaud & Van Reenen (2023): the same rules cut aggregate innovation by 5.7%.
  - Several EU digital acts use size and user thresholds (DSA, DMA, AI Act), so these findings apply directly.
- **Entry regulation:**
  - Djankov et al. (2002): heavier entry rules go with more corruption, not better outcomes.
  - Klapper, Laeven & Rajan (2006): costly entry rules reduce new firms and force entrants to start bigger.
  - Bertrand & Kramarz (2002): French retail employment could have been 10% higher without entry approvals.
  - Bruhn (2011): simplifying registration in Mexico raised firm numbers by 5% and wage employment by 2.2%.
- **Compliance costs fall hardest on small firms:**
  - Commission expert group (2007): where a large firm spends €1 per employee on a regulatory duty, a small firm spends up to €10.
  - Chambers, McLaughlin & Richards (2022): more regulation cuts the number of small firms and their employment, but not large firms'.
  - Trebbi & Zhang (2022): compliance labour averages 1.3% of the US wage bill, peaking at firms of about 500 employees.
  - Kalmenovitz (2023): higher regulatory intensity leads firms to invest and hire less, and lobby more.
  - Bailey & Thomas (2017): more-regulated industries see fewer new firms and slower job growth.
- **Aggregate US estimates** (contested):
  - Dawson & Seater (2013) and Coffey, McLaughlin & Peretto (2020) find federal regulation cut US growth substantially. The latter estimates about 0.8 points a year since 1980 (summary only).
  - Counter-evidence: Goldschlag & Tabarrok (2018) find rising federal regulation does *not* explain the decline in US start-up rates.

## 4. Regulation and innovation

- **Mixed in general:**
  - Blind (2012): effects depend on the type of regulation. Good IP regimes, product legislation and environmental law can go with *more* innovation.
  - The Porter hypothesis holds that well-designed rules can spur innovation. Ambec et al. (2013) find reasonable support for the weak version and mixed evidence for the strong version, that rules make firms more competitive.
- **Uncertainty is costly:** Stern (2017) finds pioneer medical devices spend 34% longer in approval than follow-ons, at a cost of over 7% of development cost. Delays fall once regulators publish clear guidance. This is relevant to the AI Act's reliance on standards and guidelines still being written.
- **Privacy regulation and the GDPR:**
  - Goldfarb & Tucker (2011): EU privacy rules made online ads about 65% less effective.
  - Jia, Jin & Wagman (2021): GDPR reduced EU venture deals relative to the US, by 26% in the first year (press-release figure).
  - Janssen et al. (2022): GDPR led to the exit of a third of apps and halved new app entry.
  - Johnson, Shriver & Goldberg (2023) and Peukert et al. (2022): GDPR raised concentration among web technology vendors, with Google gaining share.
  - Frey & Presidente (2024): tech firms' profits fell 2.1%, with the burden on smaller firms.
  - Johnson (2022), reviewing the field: mostly harms to firms, alongside some real privacy gains.
- **Platform power also harms innovation:**
  - Cunningham, Ederer & Ma (2021): 5–7% of pharmaceutical acquisitions are "killer acquisitions".
  - Kamepalli, Rajan & Zingales (2020): a "kill zone" around big platforms deters venture funding.
  - Both support the *rationale* for platform regulation such as the DMA.
- **The counter-view:** Bradford (2024) argues Europe's tech gap stems from its fragmented digital single market, shallow capital markets, bankruptcy law, risk aversion and talent constraints, not from strict digital rules.

## 5. Productivity, GDP and the EU–US gap

- **Product market regulation slows productivity:**
  - Nicoletti & Scarpetta (2003): competition-friendly reforms go with faster productivity growth, especially for countries far from the frontier.
  - Bourlès et al. (2013): anticompetitive rules in upstream sectors (services, network industries) curbed productivity growth downstream, most strongly near the frontier.
  - Arnold, Nicoletti & Scarpetta (2008): restrictive service regulation hampered productivity in ICT-using sectors.
  - Alesina et al. (2005): entry barriers reduce investment.
- **The ICT-era gap:**
  - Van Ark, O'Mahony & Timmer (2008): US productivity growth accelerated after 1995 while Europe's slowed, mainly in market services and ICT use.
  - Bloom, Sadun & Van Reenen (2012): US firms get more out of IT through management practices, including in Europe.
  - Andrews, Criscuolo & Gal (2016): frontier firms pull away from the rest, more so where product market reforms were limited.
  - Draghi (2024) and Schnabel (ECB, 2024) place the post-1995 gap in the tech sector. Schnabel cites strict product market regulation, rigid labour markets and red tape as obstacles to digital adoption.
- **Fragmentation versus harmonisation:**
  - The IMF (2024) estimates intra-EU barriers equal a 44% tariff on manufacturing and 110% on services (summary only; contested).
  - Mayer, Vicard & Zignago (2019): the single market raises trade more than three times as much as a typical trade agreement and is worth 3–7% of GDP.
  - The European Parliament's research service (2019) puts the potential gains from deeper integration at €2.2 trillion over ten years (summary only).
  - **Implication:** EU-level rules that *replace* 27 national regimes can cut costs, even as their volume grows.

## 6. Regulation and technology adoption: the link to the productivity gap

The productivity gap opened after 1995, when the US gained from using information technology and Europe did not. The lesson is that adopting technology matters more for growth than inventing it. Adoption is hardest for small firms, especially when regulation adds uncertainty and red tape.

- **Europe missed the ICT gains by not adopting, not by not inventing.** Gordon & Sayed (2020) find most of the 1995–2005 US productivity revival came from ICT-intensive services, such as retail, wholesale and finance, that put IT to use. Europe instead slowed "due to a paucity of ICT investment, a failure to capture the efficiency benefits of ICT", and shortfalls in those same sectors.
- **Adoption, firm growth and entry explain differences within the EU.** A JRC study (Martinez Turegano 2020) finds productivity relative to the US is higher where firms adopt technology, grow larger, and enter and exit freely. Its policy conclusions: improve technology adoption, remove barriers to firm growth, and ease entry and exit, especially in business services.
- **Data rules make data-driven technology costlier.**
  - Demirer et al. (2024): after the GDPR, EU firms store 26% less data and do 15% less computation than comparable US firms, equivalent to a 22% rise in the cost of data.
  - Janssen et al. (2022): a third of apps exited.
  - Johnson, Shriver & Goldberg (2023): web-technology vendor use fell 15%.
- **Compliance absorbs the staff and time that adoption needs.** In an IAB survey of 9,209 German establishments (Diegmann & Kubis 2025):
  - 68% name the GDPR as the regulation that burdens them most, followed by EU IT-security rules at 32%.
  - Red tape overall led firms to hire about 325,000 extra staff in three years.
  - 55% report productivity losses, 19% investment obstacles and 16% innovation obstacles.
- **Small firms bear more, especially under uncertainty; large firms cope better.** Hall & Sun (2026) show the scale of in-house policy teams at large firms: about 52 at the average Fortune 100 firm, against one or two at the average firm. Compliance costs per employee can be up to ten times higher for small firms (section 3). Unclear rules add delay: Stern (2017) finds pioneer products spend 34% longer in approval until regulators publish guidance. Together these explain why regulation can slow adoption among small firms and entrench incumbents.

**The argument, and its limits:** regulation raises the cost and uncertainty of adopting and using digital technology, especially for smaller firms. That slows the diffusion that drove US productivity growth. Each link has supporting evidence. The full chain from EU rules to the aggregate gap has not been estimated in a single study.

## What this means for reading our data

1. **Volume is not the whole story; design matters.** The literature points to entry barriers, size thresholds, uncertainty and complexity as the costly features. Those are partly visible in our counts: restrictions, the second layer of implementing acts, and growth during negotiation.
2. **Small firms bear more.** Fixed compliance costs explain why regulation can increase concentration, the opposite of what the DMA and the Data Act intend.
3. **Harmonisation has value.** An EU regulation that replaces national rules can lower costs, even if it adds restrictions at EU level.
4. **The causal link from EU digital rules to the tech gap is not established.** The firm-level evidence on the GDPR is fairly consistent; the macro claim is argued, not proven.
